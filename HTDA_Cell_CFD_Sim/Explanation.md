## 2.1 HTD/PA design
The HTD/PA was designed as a modular, automation-compatible flow-cell platform for standardized diffusion/permeation experiments (Fig. 2C). The flow-cell module was designed in Fusion 360 (Autodesk, USA) and fabricated using transparent photopolymer resin (Clear Resin V4.1, Formlabs) on a Form 3L stereolithography printer. The transparent construction enables direct visualization of liquid circulation, bubble formation, membrane positioning, and receptor-chamber filling during operation. Each HTD/PA module consists of 4-pairs of dual flow-cells, with each cell set comprising a donor compartment accommodating an interchangeable commercial permeable insert (donor - sample) and a receptor compartment, connected to a closed-loop circulation system. Standard Luer-lock connectors were incorporated at the inlet and outlet ports of each channels to facilitate connection to silicone tubing, a customed inline heat exchanger, flow sensors, and a multichannel reciprocating pump (Section 2.2). The modular architecture enables replacement of donor inserts with different geometry while maintaining a standardized receptor geometry (Flow cell I.D 2.5 cm) across experiments (Fig. 2D). A custom 3D-printed lid secures the donor inserts, minimizes dead volume above the receptor chamber, and reduce evaporation during long-term experiments. A self-sealing cross-slit silicone septum integrated into the lid allows repeated robotic pipette access for automated release sampling without exposing the receptor compartment to the environment. The HTD/PA cell geometry and mechanical design were evaluated through computational fluid dynamics to identify a design that achieves adequate receptor-side mixing across all cells of the apparatus. Steady-state CFD velocity contours of flow through the HTD/PA block cells (Ansys Fluent 2025 R2; Fig. 2E) were used to compare candidate cell geometries and select an optimized design. For the optimized cell, shear rate and vorticity were evaluated as a function of inlet flow rate (Fig. 2F); both increased with flow rate, with vorticity rising from ~3.2 s⁻¹ at 10 mL/min to ~16 s⁻¹ at 40 mL/min. As a measure of local shear and rotational flow intensity, vorticity was adopted as a practical, quantitative proxy for convective mixing within the receptor chamber. Because the multichannel reciprocating pump (Section 2.2) delivers a bipolar square-wave flow-rate waveform rather than steady unidirectional flow, mixing under this operating condition was further assessed with a transient, simplified 2D model of a single receptor cell (a rectangular chamber with inlet/outlet ports positioned as in the cup-shaped receptor geometry, with the membrane and donor compartment above), solved in Python with the NVIDIA Warp finite-element framework by imposing the bipolar flow-rate waveform at the two ports. Figure 2G shows the resulting vorticity and pressure contours at representative time points across a reciprocation cycle, capturing jet formation, flow reversal, and the associated shear layers. Peak vorticity normalized by flow-rate amplitude (Fig. 2H) rises sharply between 5 and ~20 mL/min before plateauing at higher amplitudes, indicating diminishing mixing gains beyond this threshold; this relationship was used to set the minimum pump flow-rate amplitude required for adequate receptor-side mixing without operating at unnecessarily high flow. As with the steady-state Ansys analysis, these results were used as design-supporting predictions rather than an experimentally validated description of the hydrodynamic environment.
